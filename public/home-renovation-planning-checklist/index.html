Home renovation planning checklist

Most renovation overruns start before work begins: unclear scope, hidden conditions and poor communication between office and site. Use this checklist to settle the decisions, records and sequence that protect your budget and timeline in an existing coastal Karnataka house.

Before you decide what to change

Start with the outcome, not the wish list. Write down what the house must do for you over the next ten years, who will live in it, and how long you expect to stay in the house while work is under way. Clear answers to these questions make later trade-offs much easier.

  • Main goals: the problem each change solves, such as space, light, storage, ventilation or resale appeal
  • How long you will stay in the house, and whether family members will be living there during the work
  • A budget ceiling you can defend, with a separate reserve for surprises
  • Whether the existing layout can meet your needs, or whether the changes you want will work against the structure

Renovate or rebuild? Renovation is usually the better route when the structure is sound and the layout can be adjusted within reasonable limits. Demolition and rebuilding may make more sense when the structure is in poor condition, when the layout cannot achieve your goals without major structural changes, or when the cost of upgrading wiring, plumbing and drainage approaches the cost of new construction. This is a judgement that needs a structural assessment and a costed comparison, not a rule of thumb.

Assess the existing house

A careful survey before design protects you from building new work on top of old problems. Walk through the house with a notebook and camera, and record what you see. Structural concerns need a structural engineer's assessment. Do not rely on a visual check alone to decide whether a wall, beam or slab can be altered.

  • Structural cracks: their width, direction and pattern, and whether they are recent or long standing
  • Dampness, leakage and roof condition, which matter particularly in coastal monsoon climates
  • Electrical wiring: age, condition, earthing, and whether the existing load can support new appliances
  • Plumbing and drainage: pipe material, falls, water pressure, and where stacks and outlets run
  • Termite and timber condition in doors, frames, roof members and cupboards
  • Window and door condition, including frames, hardware and how well they seal against rain
  • Existing drawings and approvals: whether the house on site matches the approved plan
  • Neighbour and access constraints: shared walls, boundary setbacks, and access for materials and waste
  • What must stay and what can change, marked clearly on a plan

Define the scope in writing

A verbal scope is one of the most common sources of disputes. Put the scope on paper before you ask for quotations, so that every contractor prices the same work.

  • A room-by-room list of work, including ceilings, walls, floors, joinery and external areas
  • Specifications and brands with grades for major items such as tiles, sanitaryware, switches, paint and waterproofing
  • What is retained, repaired or replaced, stated for each element
  • Drawings for any layout change, showing walls removed, new openings and fixture positions
  • Who supplies what: owner-supplied items, contractor-supplied items and anything you will buy directly
  • Exclusions: items that are not part of the scope, such as furniture, appliances or landscaping
  • A change-approval process: how changes are requested, priced, approved in writing and recorded

Plan budget and contingency

A renovation contingency must account for hidden conditions that only appear once walls and floors are opened. Common examples include damp in the masonry, corroded pipes, undersized or ageing wiring, and timber weakened by termites. A contingency set as a fixed percentage without this context can be too small for an old house and larger than needed for a sound one. Set it after the assessment, and review it at each milestone.

Ask for trade-wise quotations rather than one headline figure. Each quotation should show what is included, what is excluded and what assumptions have been made about the condition of the house. You can compare them using our guide to comparing construction quotations, and plan the overall split across categories with home budget allocation.

Plan the sequence and living arrangements

Renovation work follows a logical order, and that order affects cost, quality and how easily you can live on site. A typical sequence runs as follows:

  • Strip-out and inspection, with concealed conditions recorded before anything is closed up
  • Structural and waterproofing repairs
  • Services: electrical and plumbing rough-in
  • Plastering and flooring
  • Joinery and kitchen installation
  • Painting
  • Fixtures, cleaning and handover

Decide early whether you will live on site. Staying in the house is possible for some renovations, particularly where the kitchen and a bathroom can be kept usable in turn. Dust, noise and temporary loss of water or power make it hard for many households, and elderly family members or young children may need a separate place to stay.

Monsoon timing also matters. Roof, waterproofing and external plastering work is best planned for the drier months, because rain delays work and can expose open roofs or partly completed walls. Your contractor and consultants should confirm the programme in writing, but no programme can remove the risk of weather or hidden conditions.

Keep office and site on the same page

Most site disputes come from communication gaps rather than lack of effort. When decisions are made by phone, on site without drawings, or by different people on different days, the work drifts away from the design and the budget follows. Good renovation management sets up a simple record system from the first visit.

Agree on one point of contact on the owner side and one on the contractor side. Every site visit should produce written notes with dated photographs and a short list of action items, each with an owner and a deadline. Decisions and changes should be approved in writing before work proceeds, and payments should be tied to milestone reviews where completed work is checked against the scope.

  • Written site visit notes with dated photographs
  • One named point of contact on each side
  • An action list with owners and deadlines after every visit
  • Changes and selections approved in writing before the work proceeds
  • Milestone reviews completed before the next payment is released

Approvals and permissions

Some renovation work may need permission from the local authority before it starts. Structural changes, extensions, a change of use or alterations to the facade can fall under local building rules, and these depend on the location and the property records. Check the position with the relevant authority before you commit to a design or appoint a contractor. We cannot promise any approval outcome, but a complete list of proposed changes, measured drawings and existing approvals will make the process clearer. Frequently asked questions

How much does renovation cost per sq ft?

The cost depends heavily on the scope, the condition of the existing structure and the level of finish, so no single figure is reliable for every house. A written scope and an on-site assessment give a far more useful basis than a headline rate. For a wider view of how interior costs are structured, see the interior design cost guide for Karnataka.

Should I renovate or demolish and rebuild?

It depends on the structural condition, the layout limits and the cost of upgrading services. A structural engineer's assessment and a costed comparison are needed before you decide. Renovation is often sensible for a sound structure, while rebuilding may be worth considering when the building cannot meet your needs without major structural work.

Do I need an architect for a renovation?

Small cosmetic work may not need design drawings, but any change to layout, openings, services or the structure benefits from a drawn plan, specifications and a clear scope. An architect also helps coordinate structural and services consultants where they are needed.

How do I avoid extra costs during renovation?

Most extra costs come from unclear scope, late decisions, hidden conditions and changes made on site without pricing. Survey the house before design, write the scope and specifications, keep a contingency for concealed conditions, and approve every change in writing before the work proceeds.

Can I stay in the house during renovation?

Sometimes, if the work is phased and the kitchen, bathroom and sleeping areas remain usable. Major structural, plumbing or electrical work usually makes living on site difficult. Decide this early, because it affects the sequence, the budget for temporary facilities and the timeline.
