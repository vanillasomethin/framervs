Designing a duplex or home with rental units

A home that earns rent is two projects in one: a family home and a small investment. Design decisions should be tested against both.

Updated 20 November 2026 · By Vanilla & Somethin' · Architecture & Interior Design, Mangalore

Start with feasibility, not floor plans

Before sketching rooms, find out what the plot is allowed to carry. Setbacks, ground coverage, floor area, height and parking rules are set by the local authority, and they vary by zone. Confirm them for the specific plot rather than relying on a general rule of thumb or on what a neighbour built.

Work through these questions before the design starts:

  • What built-up area, height and setbacks the zone and authority allow for this plot.
  • Road width and access for construction vehicles, daily use by tenants, and emergency access.
  • Parking for the owner and for each rental unit, including space for two-wheelers.
  • The condition of any existing structure, and whether it should be demolished or partly retained.
  • A realistic budget that covers demolition, debris removal and services, not only the building.
  • Who the tenants are likely to be, since this shapes unit size, entrances and finishes.

A structural engineer should assess an existing building before anyone decides to keep it. Age alone does not settle the question. Cracks, damp, and the condition of the foundation and roof can point either way.

Common configurations

Four layouts come up most often. Each trades privacy, entry arrangement and flexibility differently, and the right choice depends on the plot, the family and the tenant market in your area.

Owner on one floor, rental on the other

The simplest split on a two-storey home. The owner and the tenant share the plot but can have separate stairs or entrances. Privacy depends on how the upper floor is reached and how well the floor is detailed for sound.

Ground-floor rental unit with separate entry

The rental has its own door from the road or compound, so tenants never pass through the family home. Entry and parking are easier to manage, but the ground floor gives up space the owner might otherwise use, and the unit needs good weather protection.

Two rental units plus owner unit

Three units on a modest plot need careful planning of staircases, water supply, bin storage and parking. Flexibility is high because units can be let separately, but every extra door and meter adds management work for the owner.

Duplex owner unit with a studio or 1BHK rental

A compact rental keeps the owner's duplex large and private. A studio or one-bedroom unit suits single tenants or couples well, though its size limits who will take it and for how long.

Design principles for rental-friendly homes

These principles help a rental unit stay practical after years of use. Several are far cheaper to build in from the start than to add later, so raise them with your architect and engineer early.

  1. Separate entrances and staircases where the plot allows, so tenants and family do not share circulation.
  2. Separate electricity and water meters for each unit, so usage and bills are clear to everyone.
  3. Acoustic separation between floors and walls, using slab detailing, insulation and well-planned service shafts.
  4. Durable, easily replaced finishes in rental units, chosen for hard use as well as appearance.
  5. Simple, efficient layouts with few awkward corners and short service runs, which are easier to repair and to re-let.
  6. Parking and two-wheeler space for every unit, planned at the start rather than squeezed in later.
  7. Storage in each unit, since tenants expect cupboards and families need the same space.
  8. Future flexibility, such as units that can be joined into one home for family use later.

Lifecycle cost matters more for rental units

A rental unit is used harder, and by people who do not own it. Repairs disrupt tenants and interrupt rental income, so the cheapest build is not always the cheapest home to keep. The coastal climate adds to this. Salt-laden air, heavy monsoon rain and humidity wear down paint, metal fittings and poorly protected concrete faster than many owners expect.

Look at these areas across the whole life of the building:

  • Waterproofing of roofs, terraces, bathrooms and other wet areas, which is hard to fix without disturbing the unit above or below.
  • Coastal weathering on external walls, windows, railings and gates, and on the metal used for them.
  • Repainting cycles, and whether finishes can be touched up without repainting whole walls.
  • Plumbing accessibility, meaning pipe shafts and inspection points that let a plumber work without breaking tiles.
  • Finishes chosen for durability and for ease of replacement.

Spending more on waterproofing, drainage and services at the start often reduces repair disruption later. Ask your architect and structural engineer to show how each service can be reached without damaging a finished unit. Specialist waterproofing and services work should be specified in writing, and the arrangements for supervising site work should be agreed before construction begins.

Budgeting a rental-unit home

A home with rental units usually costs more than a single-family house of the same built-up area. The reason is that it carries more of everything that adds cost: extra kitchens and bathrooms, additional staircases, separate water and electrical services, and, if you plan ahead, a structure designed to take another floor later.

The main cost drivers are:

  • Extra kitchens and bathrooms, each with its own plumbing, waterproofing and fittings.
  • Additional staircases and landings, which take space as well as material.
  • Separate services for each unit, including meters, pipes, wiring and drainage.
  • A structure that can carry an additional floor later, which adds cost now even if that floor is built years from now.

For an early check only, published Mangaluru references show construction packages across a wide band. For planning, you can test scenarios around illustrative indicators of ₹2,000–₹3,500 per sq ft of built-up area, then replace them with a measured design, a written specification and local quotations. These are third-party indicators, not quotes from our studio. The house construction cost guide for Karnataka sets out the sources and their limits.

Two budget lines are often forgotten. Demolition and debris removal sit outside construction, and on a plot with an older building they can be significant, especially where access is tight. Give them their own lines rather than assuming the construction rate covers them. For a wider view of how the build fits your finances, read home budget allocation alongside this guide.

Approvals and paperwork

Approvals for a rental-unit home involve several documents, and the rules differ by local authority. Typically you will need to check:

  • Building permission for the new structure, including the number of floors and units you plan to build.
  • Demolition permission for the existing house, where the authority requires it.
  • Utility connections for each unit, such as water, electricity and drainage, which may need separate applications.
  • Tenancy agreements for each rental unit, setting out rent, deposit, maintenance responsibilities and house rules.

Ask the local authority to confirm what applies to your plot before you finalise the design. A layout that works on paper can still be refused if it breaks a rule on setbacks or floor area. A tax professional should advise you on how rental income is treated and what deductions may apply, so that you are not relying on general statements from a design guide. We cannot promise approval outcomes or timelines. For help organising the paperwork, see permits and licensing support.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to demolish an old house or add a floor?

It depends on a structural assessment and on the layout you want. An engineer can say whether the existing foundation and walls can take new loads, and an architect can test whether the old plan supports separate units. Demolition often gives a cleaner layout, while retaining part of a structure saves cost only if it is sound.

Can I add a rental floor later?

You can, but only if the structure and services are designed for it now. Ask a structural engineer to design the foundation, columns and beams for future loads, and plan the stair, water and drainage routes so they can be extended without rework.

Do rental units need separate meters?

Separate meters are strongly recommended because they make usage and bills clear for both the owner and the tenants. Utility rules vary by authority and provider, so confirm the requirements for your connection before you finalise the electrical and plumbing design.

How do I keep tenants and family private?

Start with separate entrances and staircases, then add acoustic separation between floors, and locate noisy areas such as kitchens and bathrooms away from shared walls. Clear parking and a defined boundary for the rental area also help both sides feel settled.
