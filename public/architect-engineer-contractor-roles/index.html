Architect, engineer, contractor or designer: who does what on your home?

Most homeowners meet several professionals in the first months of a project, and their roles often overlap in conversation. The confusion costs money when a free plan or a combined quotation is accepted without knowing who is accountable for design, structure, approvals and workmanship. Read the responsibilities clearly before you sign anything.

Why the roles get confused

Builders offer design, designers offer execution, and some firms present a free plan alongside a construction contract. The useful question is not who is better, but who is accountable for each decision: the layout, the structural design, the specification, the quantities, the workmanship on site and the final handover.

When one party both designs and builds, the drawings and the price come from the same source. That can work well, but you should understand how the design was checked and who answers if the building does not match the drawings. When the designer is separate from the contractor, the drawings can be used to compare quotations on equal terms, although the design fee then becomes a separate line in your budget.

On the word architect: in India, the title "architect" may only be used by a person registered with the Council of Architecture under the Architects Act, 1972. Homeowners can check registration on the Council of Architecture website before appointing anyone who uses the title.

Do I need an architect if my contractor provides plans?

Contractor-provided plans can work for simple, repetitive buildings where the layout, structure and specification are standard and well understood. The right choice depends on the size, complexity and budget of your home.

The point to weigh is whose interest the drawings serve. A plan prepared by the contractor is usually designed to be priced and built by that contractor. That is not necessarily a problem, but it means the drawings may not show the detail you need to compare another contractor's quotation like for like. Items such as waterproofing details, door and window schedules, or finish specifications left out of the drawings often become variations later.

Independent design gives you a documented scope, specifications and drawings that you can send to several contractors and compare against the same brief. It also gives you a clear record of what was agreed. It does not remove the need for a structural engineer or for careful checks on site, and the design fee is a real cost to weigh against the value of a clearer brief.

Who signs what for approvals

Local authorities usually require building drawings and certificates from qualified professionals before a permit is issued, and sometimes at later stages of construction. A submission commonly includes architectural drawings, structural details and, for some projects, certificates from a licensed engineer or supervisor. Which professional signs which document depends on the local body, the size and type of building, and the rules in force when the application is made.

Responsibility for structural stability sits with the structural engineer who designs the structure, and the contractor is responsible for building to those drawings with the specified materials. Neither replaces the other, and the owner should know who has signed each drawing.

Requirements vary by local body and change over time, so confirm the current checklist with the relevant authority or your architect before you plan the timeline. No professional can promise an approval outcome or a fixed timeline for any authority. For a general overview, see our permits and licensing support page.

Questions to ask before you appoint anyone

Ask these questions in writing, and keep the answers with the agreement.

  1. Is the scope of work confirmed in writing?
  2. What deliverables will I receive at each stage, and in what format?
  3. How often will you visit the site, and who attends those visits?
  4. Who coordinates the structural, services and interior consultants?
  5. How are changes requested, approved and priced before the work is done?
  6. Who is liable for design errors, and who is liable for defects in workmanship?
  7. What is excluded from the quoted price or fee?
  8. How do payments link to milestones, and what evidence supports each payment?

How the roles work together on a typical home

On a typical home the sequence below is common. The exact order and the lead for each stage depend on the contract, and some stages overlap.

  1. Brief and feasibility: led by the owner with the architect, who checks the site, the budget and the local rules.
  2. Concept design: led by the architect, with the owner approving layout and massing.
  3. Structural and services design: led by the structural engineer and the MEP consultant, coordinated by the architect.
  4. Approvals: submitted by the architect with the engineer's and other consultants' documents, in line with local requirements.
  5. Working drawings and specifications: led by the architect, with interior drawings added by the interior designer where relevant.
  6. Tendering and contractor selection: led by the architect or quantity surveyor, with the owner deciding on the appointment.
  7. Construction: led by the contractor, with design-side site reviews by the architect and engineer at agreed stages. Site reviews are not the same as full-time supervision, so the contract should state how often they happen.
  8. Handover: led by the architect or project manager, with as-built information and operating manuals passed to the owner.

Frequently asked questions

Is an interior designer the same as an architect?

No. An architect is responsible for the building design, including layout, coordination of consultants and approval drawings, and the title is regulated in India. An interior designer works within the building, planning the interior layout, joinery, materials and finishes. Some practices offer both services, and the scope should be stated clearly in the agreement.

Can one firm do design and construction?

Yes. Design-build arrangements exist and can simplify communication. Before you agree, understand who prepares the drawings, who checks them, how the design is priced, and who is responsible if the construction does not match the design.

Who is responsible if there is a structural problem?

In general, the structural engineer is responsible for the structural design, and the contractor is responsible for workmanship to those drawings and the specified materials. The architect is responsible for coordinating the design. The contract defines these responsibilities for your project, so read the scope and liability clauses before you sign.

How do I check if an architect is registered?

Architects in India register with the Council of Architecture, and only registered persons may use the title. You can search the register on the Council of Architecture website to confirm a person's registration. Ask for the registration number in writing and check that the name matches the person who will sign your drawings.

What does a project manager add?

A project manager coordinates the consultants and contractors, tracks the programme, cost and decisions, and reports progress to you. This is useful on larger or multi-trade projects, where the owner would otherwise chase many parties. A project manager does not replace the structural engineer or the architect.
